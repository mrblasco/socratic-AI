Reviewer 4

After carefully reviewing the latest version of the manuscript, I unfortunately still feel unable to recommend this paper for publication.

Below, I outline the manuscript’s key issues that lead to this recommendation. A number of other salient weaknesses I have already identified in my previous reviews; therefore, I will not reiterate them here. In addition, there are a number of smaller issues that would require revision. However, I will not list them here because this would be appropriate for a review aimed at minor revisions, which, unfortunately, I do not consider a realistic next step.

1. Research questions, hypotheses, and theoretical background. The research questions are presented already in the Introduction section of the manuscript and the connection to the Literature Review section is hard to see. Then, the authors present their hypotheses at the end of the Methods section. And the hypotheses include several concepts that are not addressed and defined in their theoretical background, but only mentioned briefly in the Methods section. For example, the concept ‘perceived helpfulness’ (RQ3) is not properly defined prior to the formulated hypotheses (H3c).

Furthermore, several other essential constructs are not defined in the theoretical background. For example: The authors state that critical thinking is one of the central variables in the study: ‘We conducted a study across two schools to investigate the impact of different AI interaction strategies on students’ critical thinking, self-confidence, and task performance’ (p. 9). However, they first define ‘critical thinking’ in a footnote in the methods section, and they do not cite any references for it (‘In this paper, critical thinking refers to the ability to purposefully analyse, evaluate, and interpret information in order to form reasoned judgments based on evidence,’ p. 9). By contrast, chapter 2.2 ‘AI agents in education’ (p. 7) in the Literature review section appears quite abstract and provides no comprehensive definitions of the central aspects of the experiments conducted.

Finally, regarding H5, the authors refer to RQ5, which is not formulated in the paper. Therefore, the central aspects of RQ5 are also not covered in the manuscript’s theoretical background.

2. Limitations in the Methods section. I had major difficulty interpreting the results of the experiments because the Methods section does not provide an overview of the key variables measured in the study or justify the analysis approaches used. For instance, the authors write, ‘Only for task 3, we had an additional metric, which was the correctness of their answers’ (p. 10). However, task 3 is described as ‘respond to physics questions on how sound propagates in different media’ (p. 10). The authors provide no information about the type of questions (multiple choice or open-ended, etc.) or the type of scale used for the correctness assessment.

Additionally, the authors do not explain their chosen analytical approaches, such as applying non-parametric tests. Moreover, certain analytical approaches used in the Results section, such as the Mann-Whitney U test or the automatic evaluation of Socratic dimensions, are not mentioned in the Methods section, but first appear in the Results section.

3. Problematic of the ‘guessing’ task. As I mentioned in my previous reviews, I still find the interpretation of the first experiment’s results difficult due to the wording used in the instruction. In the experiment, students were asked to ‘guess’ and not to ‘estimate,’ how many coins were in a jar. The authors changed the wording in the new version of the manuscript, which I find problematic, because experimental task instructions can, obviously, not be changed retroactively, but might have an impact on the cognitive processes triggered in learners.

4. The correctness behind the analyses and authors' conclusions. I have to say that some of the results reported by the authors, as well as the conclusions they draw from them, seem problematic to me. In the current version of the manuscript, the authors still primarily report percentages related to values collected on Likert-type scales. Another example of inconvenient result reporting can be found on page 36: the results suggest that the differences between the two experimental conditions were only significant for all three task types combined (p = .025), but not for each task individually (p ≥ .095). The authors report this as, ‘This association, however, was stronger for Task 1 and Task 3 than Task 2, suggesting an association between the perceived AI’s helpfulness and the type of task’ (p. 17). However, the results for each of the tasks are not significant and cannot be compared in that way.

Another example of a problematic conclusion is when the authors state, ‘Our findings suggest that, in the present task context, AI-provided step-by-step reasoning can be more beneficial than self-generation-based learning’ (p. 19). However, the authors did not encourage participants in the first experiment to generate explanations on their own: Instead of showing participants an explanation, the authors merely showed them the solution and did not task them with self-generating such an explanation. Therefore, concluding that AI-generated explanations are more effective than self-generated ones goes beyond what the study examined.

Finally, the authors draw conclusions about learner engagement based on the number of messages exchanged with the AI tool. As I mentioned in a prior review, the use of this metric is difficult to justify as, in the ‘Socratic’-condition, learners were required to exchange messages with the AI purely due to being placed in that experimental condition. Unfortunately, the additional qualitative analysis of the dialogues provides limited insight into learners’ engagement because it focuses on analyzing the AI’s dialogical strategies rather than the cognitive processes of the learners. Therefore, I consider the authors’ conclusions about learner engagement, drawn from these analyses, unwarranted.

As I mentioned initially, these are just the major issues I see with the current version of the manuscript. As this is my third review, I, unfortunately, do not believe the paper is likely to achieve the required standard through further revisions. However, I recognize the considerable effort involved in conducting both experiments, as well as the extensive revisions the authors made. With that in mind, I highly recommend that the authors rewrite the paper from scratch, focusing primarily on the second experiment. This will enable them to achieve greater clarity and provide a more thorough description of the theoretical background, methodology, and results.
